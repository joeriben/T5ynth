akróasys

ἀ·κρό·α·σις [aˈkro.a.sis]: listening, paying attention, hearing

akróasys uses generative AI very differently: as the oscillator of a playable instrument, not as a generator that returns finished audio. The model sits where a sine or a saw would sit in the signal path, and what it resonates with is what you say: resonance with meaning.

There are two such oscillators. The T5 Oscillator reaches into a diffusion model's latent space of sound before that space has become audio. Two short impulses mark two points in it, and the controls move the point that is rendered — between them, and well past both, into regions no phrase can name: textures, transients, patterns, field recordings, everyday noises, orchestral gestures, alien voices, human emotional expressions, impossible hybrids. Reaching what a prompt cannot reach is the point of it, not a side effect. The Language-Resonant Oscillator has no such space to reach into: you describe an instrument, and a language model writes the Csound orchestra for it, so your words become the oscillator itself.

Most AI audio tools keep the model behind a render button and hand back finished audio. In akróasys, generation is not a separate AI step after the synth: the synth reaches into the model itself, and what comes out is one stage in a signal path that continues through sampler, wavetable, or granular playback, filters, envelopes, LFOs, sequencing, delay, and reverb.

akróasys is an open-source gift to the synth communities and to musicians. It tries out a different way of engaging generative AI — one that makes new things possible instead of automating existing ones, and that puts these models in the service of empowerment, not replacement: the model becomes an instrument in your hands, not a service that plays instead of you.

And akróasys is listening, too. It listens to you; to its environment, if you ask it — Resynth can capture external audio into the next generation; and to itself: Re-Prompt has the machine re-describe what it just made and play that description back into the next sound, Resynth feeds the sound itself into the next inference, and Drift with Auto-Regenerate keeps those loops turning while you play. It is a deeply recursive and reflexive instrument — most of its loops pass through the machine's own hearing.

That listening is the name. In the ancient Greek language, ἀκροάομαι is a middle verb with no ordinary active form, so hearing is not something a subject performs on an object. It happens in the between, and whoever hears is part of it. akróasis is the noun of that act — and akróasys spells the synth into it. The name replaces the earlier T5ynth, which pointed at T5, the text encoder the Stable Audio engines use to turn a phrase into control data for the audio model. Releases up to T5ynth 2.5.3 keep the old name and do not have the new oscillator; the first release called akróasys is 3.0.0.

Setup: Installing What You Need

Model Download

The T5 Oscillator requires at least one diffusion model; the LRO requires none — it needs the language model and Csound (see its chapter). Model weights are not bundled — they are downloaded separately. The Stable Audio Open engines (1.0 and Small) also need the auxiliary T5-Base text encoder (ungated, Apache-2.0), which installs automatically with the engine; Stable Audio 3 instead uses its own t5gemma encoder, fetched at install.

Open the Settings panel from the status bar. It has three tabs. Sound Models (the default) downloads the diffusion models the T5 Oscillator needs and shows per-model instructions. Language Model is the one subject the LRO depends on, installed or configured on its own page — see The Language Model below. Settings holds the machine-wide options that are not saved per preset:

  • Filter Oversampling (Off / 2× / 4×, default 2×) — applies to the Ladder and Warp nonlinear filter stages; does not affect SVF.
  • LRO Oversampling (Off / 2× / 4×, default 4×) — the rate the authored Csound runs at, relative to the host. Changing it recompiles the running orchestra.
  • Check for updates on startup — when a newer release exists, a banner with a Download button appears.
  • Record Event Log (.t5evt) — off by default; see Session Log.

Pick a model from Sound Models; the summary below tells you which to choose.

Stable Audio 3 Small Music & Small SFX

Stable Audio 3 (SA3) is Stability AI's current small-format generative family, added in T5ynth 2.1.0. Two checkpoints are available: Small Music for instrumental music and Small SFX for sound effects. They share one architecture and one text encoder and differ only in the target modality. SA3 Small Music is akróasys's default model.

SA3 is a latent diffusion transformer with a t5gemma text encoder (google/t5gemma-b-b-ul2), 44.1 kHz stereo. akróasys selects the model's published inference settings automatically: pingpong sampler, 8 steps, CFG 1.0.

Install. Select Stable Audio 3 Small Music or Small SFX in Settings and click Download. akróasys first shows a license dialog; you must accept both the Stability AI Community License (the audio model) and the Google Gemma Terms of Use with the Gemma Prohibited Use Policy (the t5gemma text encoder). The download then runs without a HuggingFace account or token, and the full license texts are written into the model folder. If you already downloaded the files from Stability by hand, use Auto-Scan instead.

Prompting. The t5gemma encoder reads up to 256 tokens per impulse. akróasys prepends the model's required modality prefix (TrackType: Music, VocalType: Instrumental, or TrackType: SFX,) automatically. SA3 was trained on comma-joined, field-tagged metadata (for example Instruments: …, Moods: …), so structured Field: value prompts fit its training distribution; plain descriptions also work.

Duration. SA3 generates variable-length audio; with an SA3 model selected the Duration slider reaches 120 s. For the other engines it caps at 11 s.

Stable Audio 3 Medium

Stable Audio 3 Medium is the larger SA3 checkpoint (about 2.3 billion parameters, ~9.2 GB download), added in T5ynth 2.4.0. Unlike the Small tiers, a single Medium checkpoint renders both modalities — Music or SFX is chosen per generation by the engine slot, not by a separate download. It uses the same google/t5gemma-b-b-ul2 text encoder, 44.1 kHz stereo, and the same automatic inference settings as Small (pingpong sampler, 8 steps). Internally it is a deeper diffusion transformer (24 blocks against 20, with differential attention) on a SAME autoencoder with sliding-window attention, and it needs roughly 16 GB of unified memory or VRAM to run, against about 8 GB for Small.

Install. Select Stable Audio 3 Medium in Settings and click Download. As with the Small tiers you must accept both the Stability AI Community License (the audio model) and the Google Gemma Terms of Use with the Gemma Prohibited Use Policy (the t5gemma text encoder); the download then runs login-free and the license texts are written into the model folder. Medium shares the t5gemma encoder with the Small tiers, so if you already have an SA3 Small model installed only the audio checkpoint is fetched.

Choosing Small or Medium. The tier is a per-machine choice, not part of a preset, and it defaults to Small so installing Medium never silently takes over a lighter machine. When both tiers are installed, the active SA3 model button carries a small s/m marker; click that button again — a second click on the already-selected SA3 slot — to toggle between Small and Medium. If only Medium is installed it is used automatically and the marker is hidden. Prompting and the 120 s Duration range are identical to the Small tiers.

Stable Audio Open 1.0

Stable Audio Open 1.0 is the original full-size Stable Audio Open checkpoint, licensed under the Stability AI Community License. Like Stable Audio Open Small, it also needs the auxiliary T5-Base text encoder (ungated, Apache-2.0), which installs automatically with the engine.

Install. Select Stable Audio Open 1.0 in Settings and click Download. akróasys first shows the license dialog; accept it to continue. The ~4.85 GB checkpoint then downloads without a HuggingFace account or token, and a copy of the license is written into the model folder. If you already downloaded the files from Stability by hand (model.safetensors and model_config.json), use Auto-Scan instead.

Stable Audio Open Small

The compact, fastest Stable Audio Open checkpoint, licensed under the Stability AI Community License. In Settings, select Stable Audio Open Small and click Download. akróasys shows the license dialog, then fetches the ~1.68 GB checkpoint without a HuggingFace account or token — from an ungated mirror that redistributes the official weights unmodified — and writes a license copy into the model folder. The required T5-Base text encoder (ungated, Apache-2.0) installs automatically in the same step. If you already downloaded the files from Stability by hand, use Auto-Scan instead.

AudioLDM2

AudioLDM2 is an academic latent-diffusion text-to-audio model published by the Centre for Vision, Speech and Signal Processing (CVSSP) at the University of Surrey together with collaborators; see the paper "AudioLDM 2: Learning Holistic Audio Generation with Self-supervised Pretraining" (Liu et al., 2023). It was released as an open research artefact to study generalised audio, music, and speech generation from text, not as a commercial product. It is ungated on HuggingFace and akróasys can download it directly — in Settings, select AudioLDM2 and click Download from HuggingFace.

The Language Model

One language model serves the whole instrument: it writes the LRO's Csound, translates non-English prompts, and drives Re-Prompt on both oscillators. It has its own Language Model tab in Settings, and the Author engine choice there decides where the writing happens:

  • Local (the default). Gemma 4 12B — a 4-bit quantization-aware build of Google's Gemma run through llama.cpp, about 7 GB, downloaded ungated from HuggingFace under Apache 2.0, no account needed. It works on a 16 GB machine, and nothing you type ever leaves this computer.
  • External API. If your machine cannot spare the local model, pick a provider by name — OpenRouter (US, free-tier models available), Mistral (EU), IONOS (EU, Berlin), Mammouth (EU), Anthropic (US), OpenAI (US), an Ollama server on this computer (no key needed), or "Other" for any OpenAI-compatible endpoint, including your own remote llama.cpp server. Choosing a provider fills in its address and a working model; you paste an API key. Only the text step travels — the sound is always rendered on your machine, and the Csound is compiled on your machine. With a provider configured, the tab keeps a running readout of what the authoring has cost you so far. Data protection is your call and you make it at the provider, not here; IONOS and Mammouth are the EU-hosted, GDPR-compliant entries. A code-trained model authors far better than a general chat one, and the key is stored unencrypted in akróasys's settings file, like every other machine-wide setting.

Csound

Nothing to install. Csound travels inside akróasys on macOS, Windows and Linux alike, and the compiler that checks each LRO orchestra before it is loaded is the same one that plays it. It is linked dynamically, as its licence requires, so the shipped library stays replaceable.

Its one setting is LRO Oversampling on the Settings tab (Off / 2× / 4×, default 4×): the rate the authored orchestra runs at relative to your host. Changing it recompiles the running instrument.

Device Selection

akróasys auto-detects the best available compute device:

  • Apple Silicon Mac → MPS (Metal Performance Shaders), ~3× faster than CPU
  • NVIDIA GPU on Linux or Windows 11 → CUDA
  • No GPU → CPU fallback (works, but slow)

The device toggle in the T5 Oscillator column overrides the choice: GPU or CPU.

First Generation

On first launch, the status bar shows backend connection status. Once you see "Ready", enter a sound marker in Impulse A and click Generate. The first generation takes longer because the model is loaded into GPU memory on demand. Subsequent generations are faster.

If the status bar shows an error, check: (1) Is at least one model downloaded? (2) Is there enough disk space and RAM/VRAM? Stable Audio Open 1.0 needs ~4 GB VRAM.
Watch your levels. A generative instrument is unpredictable by design: a generation can come back loud, dense, or resonant, and the Ladder and Warp filters self-oscillate at high resonance. Keep headroom on your monitoring level while exploring, especially on headphones.
The Two Oscillators

Chapters 1–6 are the T5 Oscillator and the controls that belong to it alone; chapters 7–8 are the LRO and the controls that belong to it. From chapter 9 on, everything is shared — whichever oscillator is making the sound, it is played through the same synth.

1. The T5 Oscillator

The T5 Oscillator plays a diffusion model's latent space. Two short impulses are read by the model's text encoder and become two points in that space; every control in this chapter moves the point that is actually rendered. What comes back is a short fragment, and that fragment is the oscillator's waveform — it goes on into the sampler, wavetable or granular engine, the filter, the envelopes, and the rest of the synth.

The two impulses: A & B

The two text fields at the top of the column are labelled Impulse A and Impulse B. Each one is read by the model's text-reading stage — the "T5" this oscillator is named after — and becomes a point in the latent space. The two are equal in rank: together they mark out the stretch of that space this oscillator plays across.

Each engine opens a different learned space, so the same words land somewhere else on each. The Stable Audio engines answer best to English, sound-oriented phrases — sound effects, field recordings, drum and instrument loops, ambient beds, foley, production elements — and read up to 256 tokens per impulse on SA3.

The impulses do not have to be narrow "sound ideas". They can evoke sound sources, materials in motion, acoustic scenes, bodies, gestures, moods, fictional agents, or impossible combinations. A visual phrase such as "a rose in a vase" is not a sound by itself; "water in a glass vase, quiet room, petals brushed by fingers" gives the model acoustic handles. But the abstract phrase can still be used as a strange marker in the model's space. akróasys is where you find out what that marker can become.

Leaving a field empty

An empty field is a decision, not a gap. akróasys reads the empty side as an echo chamber for the impulse that is there: a reflection of it through the model's neutral point (2·neutral − other). The A/B slider then runs from your impulse, through that neutral midpoint, out to the impulse's antipode — sounds it actively is not, and which no wording of your own would produce. Leave both fields empty and both sides are the neutral point itself: the model renders its own self-portrait, different with every seed.

To pin one character regardless of where the slider sits, type the same text in both fields — 0.5·A + 0.5·A = A at every position.

They remain real parameters, so a DAW can automate them — a value set that way holds until the next time you switch engines.

What the latent space is

A diffusion model is trained on audio paired with text, and what it learns is a latent space: an internal coordinate system in which a sound is a point rather than a waveform. Nothing in it was designed as a control surface. Its coordinates are not brightness, pitch, or attack time; they are whatever directions the training data made useful for telling sounds apart. Nearby points render as related sounds, distant ones as unrelated. Text is how you get in: the encoder maps a phrase to a point, and rendering starts there.

That is what the T5 Oscillator plays. Its controls do not act on the audio and do not act on your wording — they act on the point, before anything has been rendered. The A/B slider moves along the line through A and B (and past both ends), the Sound-Character Axes push along directions the model ties to a word, the Dimension Explorer re-weights single coordinates, Magnitude scales the whole vector, and Noise perturbs it.

Most of the travel those controls allow leads out of the region any text can address. The A/B slider runs to ±2, so most of its range lies past both impulses; an empty field reflects the other one through the model's neutral point, which is an anti-prompt no wording produces; Magnitude reaches 5.0. The model was not trained on what it finds there, and that is what this oscillator is for. The usual warning — "the model has not seen this" — describes the target here, not a fault. The one real limit is collapse: pushed far enough the model stops producing structure at all, and you hear it immediately.

All of these — A/B, the axes, Noise, Magnitude — can be modulated by Drift & Regenerate, which moves the point continuously and re-renders as it travels, so the source becomes a path through the space instead of one fixed fragment.

Injection Modes (research)

Above the A/B slider sits a row of six small buttons — Linear · Step-in · Layer · Combo 1 · Combo 2 · Combo 3. These select different ways Impulse B enters Impulse A inside the diffusion pipeline. The classical A↔B movement is one of six options — you can think of it as the question "where in the generation process should B enter?". Different answers produce audibly different sounds from the same impulses and seed.

Clicking a mode button immediately triggers a fresh generation, and each mode keeps its own slider position so A/B-ing the modes by clicking buttons does not disturb the others. Every Stable Audio engine implements the non-linear modes; with AudioLDM2 selected, akróasys disables those buttons and keeps Linear active.

ModeWhat it doesSlider role
Linear The classical A/B movement through the latent space, and beyond it by extrapolation. The default, and the best-understood of the six. α ∈ [−1, +1], A↔B with extrapolation past the poles.
Step-in Early sampler steps see Impulse A only; after a transition step the cross-attention conditioning swaps to a Step-in-controlled blend of A and B. The result has the broad shape of A and the refinement / texture of B (or a controlled mix). This mode lets B colour the late detail of A without dominating its overall envelope. Intensity 0–1: 0 = barely audible late blend, 1 = early transition + pure B in the late phase.
Layer The 16 cross-attention layers of the diffusion transformer are split into a B-zone defined by a two-thumb range slider. Layers inside the zone see Impulse B; layers outside see Impulse A. Different layer ranges produce qualitatively different mixes (low layers → surface texture, mid layers → structural conditioning). Two-thumb range slider 0–16, defining the B-zone start and end.
Combo 1 Step phase × layer band, preset to "B as surface skin": B injected only into the lowest 4 cross-attention layers, only in the late sampling phase. A relatively subtle variation — the bulk of the sound is still A, but the surface character is coloured by B. Intensity 0–1, Step-in-style coupled mapping.
Combo 2 Step phase × layer band, preset to "broad mid": B injected into 8 mid-range layers (4..11) in the late phase. The most distinct of the three Combos in empirical tests — the mid layers carry most of the diffusion's conditioning work, so a strong B presence there reshapes the result substantially. Intensity 0–1, Step-in-style coupled mapping.
Combo 3 Step phase × layer band, preset to "narrow center": B injected into 4 high-leverage center layers (6..9) in the late phase. Same width as Combo 1 but a different vertical position, probing whether where in the layer stack matters as much as how wide. Intensity 0–1, Step-in-style coupled mapping.
The slider relabels itself. It reads A ↔ B in Linear, Step-in: A → mix or Combo 1/2/3: A → mix in those modes, and Layer B-zone (low–high) in Layer, where it becomes a two-thumb range. The label is always what the slider is currently doing.
Drift LFOs targeting A/B automatically follow the active mode's intensity axis: in Linear the A/B position, in Step-in and the Combos the intensity, in Layer the band's position along its width. The drift system stays mode-aware without any extra UI.

2. Sound-Character Axes

Below the A/B area, three dropdown slots let you apply sound-character axes — continuous sliders between two conceptual poles (in the UI and technical background also called semantic axes). Each one pushes the current latent space along a direction the model ties to a word like grainy or rhythmic. That push has two sides. It is a reliable way to bring coherent, non-chaotic movement into the sound — it steers along one consistent direction, so the motion is repeatable rather than scattered. But it is not a literal control over the named quality: pushing toward grainy reliably shifts the character without reliably making the sound measurably grainier.

They are probes as much as controls. The same push lands differently on different prompts: one follows the label, another barely moves, a third veers somewhere you did not predict. That is not a fault — it is the model showing how it hears the word in the context of your sound. Pushing an axis and listening to where a prompt goes, and where it refuses to go, is a direct way to learn where the model's sense of a word lines up with yours and where it parts from it. Treat a labeled axis as a broad direction to explore by ear, not as a value you dial in.
The available axes depend on the selected model. Stable Audio Open 1.0, Stable Audio Open Small, and AudioLDM2 share the eight axes listed first below; SA3 offers a different set of three, because its t5gemma encoder has a different embedding geometry and needs its own poles. 3. Dimension Explorer

The Dimension Explorer gives you direct access to all 768 internal control dimensions that steer the model. While the A/B text fields and sound-character axes are readable handles, the DimExplorer lets you see and manipulate the raw numbers.

What You See

After generating audio, akróasys receives the 768-dimensional source-control vectors for your A/B markers. The DimExplorer displays these as a row of vertical bars — one per dimension, sorted by magnitude (the most significant dimensions appear on the left).

  • Each bar shows the difference between Source A and Source B for that dimension. Green bars point toward A, orange bars toward B. The leftmost bars are the dimensions where the two sources differ most — editing here affects the A/B contrast. The rightmost bars are where A and B are nearly identical — editing here changes what both share, often producing more fundamental sonic shifts.
  • If one text field is empty, the absent side is the Echoraum reflection of the present one (2·neutral − other), not a zero vector. The orange/green bars then read as opposing in a structured way: dimensions where the present marker is most articulate are the dimensions where the echo opposes it most strongly.
  • If both fields are empty, A and B are both the neutral encoding, so all bars collapse — there is no A↔B contrast to display.

What You Can Do

Drag any bar vertically to apply a per-dimension offset. Edited bars turn blue. Click "Apply + Generate" to send these offsets to the inference engine, where they are added directly to the internal generation controls used by diffusion.

What This Means

This is the most granular level of control akróasys offers. It bypasses language entirely — you are directly sculpting the 768-dimensional control vector that guides the diffusion model. What each dimension "does" sonically is largely opaque: the model was not built as a documented audio control surface. Small changes may do nothing, or they may radically alter the output. This is a research tool for probing the space, not a precision instrument.

A compact mini-view of the DimExplorer is always visible below the Axes panel. Click it to open the full interactive overlay. The overlay provides Undo, Redo, and Reset All controls for your edits.

4. Engine Modes: Sampler, Wavetable & Granular

A generated fragment is not yet a playable voice. These three modes are how it becomes one — three different answers to what should happen when you press a key that is not the key it was rendered at.

The waveform display

The waveform strip under the engine-mode buttons shows the loaded fragment and carries three handles on its baseline, which every mode reads:

HandleWhat it sets
S (filled)The start position — where a note begins playing.
[ and ]The play region: everything outside the brackets is not played, and in Loop and Ping-Pong modes these are the loop points.

A pale vertical line runs through the display while a note sounds, showing the live read position — useful when a modulator is driving Scan. The LOCK button in the corner pins the three handles so a new generation cannot move them. Dragging from the waveform onto a step's one-shot slot copies the region between the brackets into that slot.

Sampler Mode

Plays the generated audio buffer directly. Pitch follows MIDI via time-stretching (Signalsmith Stretch). This mode preserves the full character of the generation.

  • Loop Mode — One-shot, Loop, or Ping-Pong
  • Crossfade — 0–500 ms equal-power crossfade at the loop boundary
  • Opt (Loop Optimize) — cross-correlation search for the smoothest loop point. Click to cycle Off → Low → High; High searches harder and costs more.
  • Norm (Normalize) — on by default. Levels the play region; see below.

Wavetable Mode

Extracts pitch-synchronous frames from the audio to build a scannable wavetable. The Scan slider morphs between frames, and pitch follows MIDI directly (band-limited harmonic synthesis).

  • Scan (0.0–1.0) — Selects the current frame position in the wavetable. Can be modulated by LFOs, envelopes, or drift.
  • Frames (32 / 64 / 128 / 256, default 256) — How many frames are extracted from the fragment. Fewer frames means bigger steps between them and a coarser scan.
  • Smooth (on by default) — Interpolates between neighbouring frames instead of switching at frame boundaries.
  • Auto Scan (on by default) — Sweeps the scan position through the table on its own as a note is held, so a wavetable moves without a modulator assigned to it.

Granular Mode

Holds a small, smooth region of the generated audio and makes it playable as a sustained texture. It stays close to the source sample rather than spraying random grains. Scan selects the held position and can move slowly through modulation.

  • Texture — Hold, Silk, Air, or Cloud. These are fixed musical profiles for grain length, overlap, motion, and blur.
  • Stereo — Width control for subtle per-grain left/right offset and balance. At 0% Granular stays centered.

Noise Generator

Alongside the sampler/wavetable/granular source each voice has an independent noise generator that is summed into the voice signal before the filter. It follows the voice's amplitude envelope and note-off just like the main source, so notes including a noise component decay cleanly.

ParameterRangeNotes
Noise Level0.0 – 1.0Mix of the noise generator into the voice signal. 0 disables the noise oscillator entirely.
Noise TypeWhite / Pink / BrownWhite is flat-spectrum (xorshift64 PRNG). Pink rolls off at −3 dB/oct (Paul Kellet approximation). Brown rolls off at −6 dB/oct (leaky integrator). A one-pole DC blocker at ~5 Hz is always applied.

Generated Audio Processing

Before the generated audio reaches the sampler/wavetable/granular source, two filters and one optional gain stage are applied to the raw VAE output:

  • Rumble filter — always on, 2nd-order Butterworth high-pass at 25 Hz. Removes the DC offset and sub-bass rumble the VAE decoder tends to add. Not user-switchable; this is a baseline cleanup step.
  • HF (toggle, in Sampler controls) — two-band high shelf (+3 dB @ 4 kHz, +4.5 dB @ 10 kHz) that compensates the high-frequency rolloff inherent to the VAE decoder. Whether you prefer the brighter boosted or the darker raw sound is a matter of taste; akróasys stores both.
  • Norm (toggle, in Sampler controls, on by default) — levels the play region, and reads the signal before deciding how. Near-silent material is left alone. Material that is already hot is only peak-capped. Short or sparse material is brought to −10 dBFS at its 99.9th-percentile peak; sustained material to −18 dBFS active RMS. Everything is capped at −1 dBFS peak. It turns loud generations down as readily as it lifts quiet ones, which is what makes a session of wildly different generations playable at one monitoring level.

All three operate on the generated buffer, not on the real-time voice signal: re-toggling HF or Norm rebuilds the playback buffer from the retained raw audio, on a background thread, without calling the model again. Norm applies in Sampler and Freeze; the button is not shown in Wavetable or LRO mode.

Voice Count

Mono, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 64 or 128 voices; the default is 8. Gain auto-scales per voice count, and the oldest notes are stolen when the limit is reached. The two large settings are there for dense generative sequencing and long release tails, not for keyboard play.

5. Drift & Regenerate

Drift & Regenerate keeps the latent space moving.
Without it, you shape one space, generate one fragment, and then process that fragment with conventional synth tools. With Drift & Regenerate, the latent space itself evolves. The modulation system shifts generation parameters over time, triggering new inference runs in the background. The source is no longer a static waveform — it follows a changing path through the model's latent space.

The Asynchronous Feedback Loop

Generation is not tied to the audio callback. Depending on model, duration, step count, and hardware, a regeneration can arrive in roughly 0.1 seconds on an RTX 6000-class GPU or take several seconds on a Mac M-series processor without a dedicated AI-capable graphics card. akróasys creates an asynchronous feedback loop between the modulation system and the latent space that keeps the instrument playing while new fragments are rendered in the background:

While the synth is playing, multiple drift LFOs continuously and independently shift A/B and the Semantic Axes. Each LFO moves at its own rate and depth — A/B might slowly sweep from A toward B over 30 seconds, while Axis 1 (for example tonal/noisy) oscillates on a different cycle, and Axis 2 (for example sustained/rhythmic) drifts at yet another pace. With VAR on none or last the seed is held and this remains reproducible. With VAR on auto, each regeneration receives a new seed: the exact sound will not repeat, but the semantic poles keep the material inside the marked space.

When an auto regen mode is active, akróasys monitors how far the modulated generation controls have drifted from the last generated state. Once the drift exceeds a threshold, it fires a new inference request in the background. The current sound keeps playing uninterrupted. When the new generation arrives, it is crossfaded into the playback buffer, replacing the old sound with the new one without stopping playback.

The result is a continuous stream of new generations, each one produced from a slightly different internal state between and around the two impulses. With VAR on auto, no generation is simply repeated; on none or last the same seed and same state remain recallable. In both cases, the movement is not arbitrary: it follows the trajectories defined by the drift LFO settings inside the sound space opened by the two impulses.

Meanwhile, the conventional synth chain (filter, envelopes, LFOs, effects, sequencer) continues to process the output in real time. You are simultaneously shaping the sound and the process that creates the sound.

The Drift section provides three slow LFOs (0.001–2.0 Hz) designed for organic, evolving textures. Unlike the main LFOs, drift can target generation-level parameters — A/B, the three sound-character axes, Noise, and Magnitude. This is what makes drift unique: it doesn't just modulate how the sound is played back, it modulates what sound is generated.

Resynth as a drift target only takes effect with an SA3 model — it routes drift into the audio-conditioned re-inference loop (see § Resynth). Without SA3, the Resynth slider is disabled; the drift-target dropdown entry remains selectable but has no effect.

Re-Prompt: the semantic self-listening loop

Drift moves the generation through its parameters. Re-Prompt moves it through language: after each render the machine listens to its own output and rewrites the prompt before the next one. It is a second feedback loop, riding on the same trigger as Auto-Regenerate, but closing through words instead of control values.

Each step runs two models. A machine-listening model (CLAP, laion/clap-htsat-unfused) hears the rendered audio and ranks it against a fixed tag vocabulary — it does not describe the sound, it ranks it — yielding a handful of timbre tags plus a few signal-level spectral words (dark/bright, thin/full-bodied, tonal/textured). Those words are handed to the same local instruct LLM that powers prompt translation (Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct), which rewrites the prompt under the stance you chose. The next render is then triggered the usual way — by Generate, or automatically when an Auto-Regen mode is active — so the loop only steps when there is fresh audio to listen to. CLAP is fetched automatically from Hugging Face the first time you engage a stance (about 1 GB), so the first Re-Prompt step needs an internet connection; the interpreter is the same local model akróasys installs for prompt translation.

The point is not to "improve" the sound, and not to correct the model's listening. It is to make that listening — and your steering of it — audible. You set two things: how the machine re-reads what it hears (the stance), and how far it may displace your own words (the coupling).

The stance bar selects the interpreter's attitude. Each stance is named for the kind of motion it tends to produce over repeated steps:

Energy awareness: Re-Prompt adds a CLAP analysis and one or two short LLM passes to every regeneration, on top of the diffusion render. With a fast regen cadence it keeps re-rendering to feed itself; the same bar-limited regen modes keep that in check.

6. Resynth

Re-Prompt closes the loop through language: the machine listens to its own output and rewrites the words. Resynth closes it through audio. Instead of starting a generation from pure noise, the model starts from a waveform you hand it and denoises away from there. The prompt still conditions the render; the audio decides where the denoising begins, and therefore how much of the fed-in sound survives into the result.

The control is one slider labeled RESYNTH in the Generation column, directly below the SNAP / CACHE row, with an int / ext button pair to its right that selects which audio goes in. There is no separate on/off switch — the left end of the slider is off.

Resynth requires an SA3 model. The slider and both source buttons are enabled only while a Stable Audio 3 model is selected; on any other model they are disabled and dimmed, and the request drops the audio seed even if a preset restored a non-zero amount. SA3 is the engine whose generation entry point accepts an initial waveform. In LRO mode the row is switched off and dimmed as well.

The Resynth amount

The slider runs from Off to Full in steps of 0.05. Six positions have a word read-out; any other step shows a percentage instead. The value sets where the denoising starts (0.90 − 0.85 × amount): a high starting point means the render is mostly re-made and the prompt dominates, a low one means it stays close to the audio you fed in.

Read-outValueDenoising starts at

The direction is the opposite of what "more effect" usually means: turn Resynth down to let a typed prompt win, up to resynthesise and preserve the incoming sound.

With a Re-Prompt stance engaged, the starting point is forced to 0.9 for every render regardless of the slider, so the rewritten words are not drowned by the carried audio. The slider then decides only whether audio is attached at all, not how strongly it holds.

int and ext: which audio is fed in

SourceWhat it feeds in
int (default)The last generation — the raw model output, before HF Boost. Each render becomes the seed for the next one, so the material moves on its own.

int only attaches when a generation already exists. The first generation of a session has nothing to feed back and renders text-only.

ext takes a window as long as the current Duration setting, capped at 12 seconds — a longer duration still gets a 12-second seed, which the backend pads out. The capture is peak-normalised before it is sent, so playing in quietly does not weaken the effect. A mono input is copied to both channels. If the window is effectively silent — no input device, denied microphone permission, nothing playing — nothing is attached and the generation falls back to text-only rather than seeding silence.

The ext button stays disabled until an input is actually available: the plugin needs an open input bus and a running audio device. In the Standalone that means selecting an input in its audio settings and granting microphone permission. The button re-enables by itself once that is the case — no reload needed.

Resynth in the drift loop

Drift can target Resynth. At Depth 1.0 the offset spans ±0.5; it is added to the slider value and clamped to the 0–1 range. The drifted value shows as the orange ghost indicator on the RESYNTH row and is the amount the next regeneration uses.

Resynth also acts as a standing trigger for auto-regeneration: with an SA3 model, a Resynth amount above zero and any Regen Mode other than manual, akróasys keeps regenerating at the chosen cadence without any control having to move. Each round re-feeds the audio; when that audio changes from one round to the next, the sound evolves on its own — see the seed note below for when it does not. In manual mode nothing auto-regenerates, but Resynth still applies to every Generate you press.

One round in that loop behaves differently. When you move a generation control — A/B, an axis, Noise, Magnitude — or edit a prompt, the audio seed is detached for that single regeneration, so the new content renders cleanly from the prompt instead of being pulled back toward the carried waveform. The next round re-attaches at your setting and the loop continues from the new material. While the loop runs, the effective amount has a floor of 0.05.

Seed: when a Resynth loop repeats itself

The VAR switchbox, to the right of Duration, decides which seed each generation uses: none (the default) sends a fixed base seed, last reuses the previous generation's seed, and auto sends a fresh random seed every time. The backend draws a new seed only when the request asks for one, which is what auto does; the other two settings pin it.

The Resynth loop does not change this — each round it sets the Resynth amount and leaves the seed to VAR. With none or last the seed is therefore the same every round, and a fixed seed with an unchanging input renders the same audio. That is what none means ("no variation"), not a fault; it becomes noticeable in two cases:

  • A silent or stopped ext source. When the captured window is effectively silent, no audio is attached and the round renders text-only from the prompt. With a pinned seed that text-only render is identical every round.
  • A high Resynth amount. Near Full the denoising starts very late (Full → 0.05), so the incoming sound dominates the diffusion and successive renders come out the same — here it is the input holding the result, not the seed.

A live, audible ext input is not captured identically from round to round: the capture window is always moving and is levelled to its own peak, so at a low or medium Resynth amount the render keeps changing even with a pinned seed. With int the fed-back audio is the previous output and changes each round, so the loop generally keeps moving; only at very high Resynth amounts can it settle onto one sound.

If a loop stops evolving and you want it to move, set VAR to auto so every round draws a fresh seed. This helps most at low to medium Resynth, where prompt and seed still shape the render; the further right the slider sits, the less it changes, because the incoming sound — not the seed — is then deciding the result.
A full inference cache is a separate reason a loop repeats. Once the cache is full, Generate and auto-regeneration play back the stored results in turn instead of running the model, so the loop cycles a fixed set of sounds regardless of seed or source. Clear or resize the cache to generate fresh audio again.

7. The Language-Resonant Oscillator (LRO)

The Language-Resonant Oscillator, or LRO, is an alternative to the denoising paradigm. A diffusion model makes sound by denoising: it starts from noise and settles into a plausible rendering of what was asked. The LRO makes sound by writing software: you describe an instrument in words, and a language model — local by default, or an external provider you pick in Setup — writes Csound synthesis code from that description. Compiled and run live, that code becomes the oscillator's voice for every note you play. What is stored, recalled and played is the source code itself, written for this one prompt.

The code is real. What the LRO writes is genuine, portable Csound — the same language any Csound system compiles, not an internal format of this app. The orchestra is stored with your preset, and the panel shows it (hold the panel to read the code). And because every authoring is a fresh piece of writing, the oscillator you keep can be one of a kind: a real digital instrument that exists nowhere else.

Switching to the LRO

The toggle at the top right of the Generation column's header switches between the two oscillator paradigms. It reads » LRO while the neural T5 Oscillator is active — click it to switch. The column header itself then changes to Language-Resonant Oscillator, and the toggle now reads » T5osc to switch back.

Switching replaces the whole Generation column: Impulse A/B, the A/B slider, the Semantic Axes and the Dimension Explorer are gone, and in their place sit a single prompt field, the authoring trace described below, and a Re-Prompt card of its own. The shared Generate button and the SNAP slots below it stay in exactly the same place and now drive the LRO instead; Cache and Resynth, both meaningful only for the neural generator, are shown dimmed rather than removed.

Type a description into the prompt field at the top of the panel — its placeholder reads "Describe the oscillator to craft" — and press Generate. A single tab above the prompt field names the language model that will do the writing; it is installed or configured in Setup.

The toggle pulses gently when the column you are not looking at is holding a non-default value that still shapes what you hear — a Semantic Axis left standing while the LRO is showing, or Dimension Explorer offsets while the T5 Oscillator is. Switch over, and either reset the control or accept it; the pulse stops either way.

A written instrument brings its own knobs, and they appear in the Synth panel where the engine-mode controls sit for the other oscillator. The wiring to the Csound channel is made by the host from the line that survived into the body, so every knob on screen moves something in the running orchestra.

  • Warp — zero-delay-feedback 4-pole ladder with a selectable per-pole saturation curve (the Style control). Good for shaping large continuous timbral spaces, which is why it's the recommended target when mapping an internal control dimension to filter character. All six styles are centered at zero, so the feedback loop stays stable under modulation. Warp is the most expensive of the three (~2–3× SVF CPU). Design inspiration: Surge XT Cutoff Warp; akróasys's implementation is original.
Warp styles — all bounded, all with sat(0) = 0 so the feedback loop has a clean zero equilibrium:
  • Tanh — symmetric soft, the classic reference.
  • SoftClip — x / (1 + |x|); gentler, flatter knee than tanh.
  • Algebraic — x / √(1 + x²); slower approach to the limit than tanh with more odd-harmonic content for a given drive.
  • Sin — wavefolded sine; FM-like timbre when driven hard.
  • Digital — hard clamp to ±1; deliberately buzzy, rich in odd harmonics at any drive.
  • Asym — tanh(x + 0.25) − tanh(0.25); asymmetric response, prominent 2nd-harmonic colouring, bias-compensated so it's still centered at zero.

11. Step Sequencer

A traditional step sequencer of 2 to 32 steps, 16 by default. Each step has:

  • Note — MIDI note 0–127 (draggable vertical slider)
  • Velocity — 0.0–1.0 (bar at bottom)
  • Enable — toggle the step on/off
  • Bind / Glide — one button, cycling Off → Bind → Glide → Off. Bind suppresses the note-off so the step ties into the next one; Glide ties it and ramps the pitch across, over the global Glide Time. Glide is drawn brighter than Bind.
  • One-shot sample slots — three small per-step slots for drum-style sample triggers

Drag a step's note lane to set its pitch silently. Hold Shift while clicking or dragging the note lane to audition the step as a held preview note.

Recording Steps (Live Entry)

Instead of dragging each note lane, you can play a pattern in. Double-click the Step mode button to arm recording: it turns red and reads REC, and the step waiting for input is framed in red. While armed, every note you play — on a connected MIDI keyboard or the enabled computer keyboard — is written into the current step (its pitch, plus velocity from a MIDI keyboard; the computer keyboard records a fixed velocity) and the cursor advances to the next step. Recording is monophonic: one note per step, so a chord played as a roll lands across consecutive steps in the order struck.

To leave a step empty (a rest), press Space on the computer keyboard or tap the sustain pedal on a MIDI keyboard — the current step stays silent and the cursor still moves on. While recording, the sustain pedal enters rests rather than holding notes; outside recording it is the normal damper.

A recorded note plays back at the pitch you played, independent of the Octave Shift setting. Recording works whether or not the transport is running. Reaching the last step stops further writing; double-click the Step button again — or switch to GEN — to end recording. Each time you re-arm, the cursor restarts at step 1.

Per-Step One-Shot Slots

Each step can hold up to three independent one-shot samples. Drag from the waveform area onto a slot to copy the current P1→P3 region into that slot. The copied audio is a physical sample owned by the step sequencer, not a reference to the current main buffer, so later generations do not overwrite it.

Fix Toggles (Lock Icons)

Each Euclidean parameter (Steps, Pulses, Rotation, Mutation) has a lock toggle. When unlocked, that parameter itself drifts over time — the pattern length, density, rotation, and mutation rate all evolve. Lock what you want stable, unlock what you want to breathe.

Core Monitor

The Strand 1 module slot holds the Core Monitor, a read-only, phosphor-green readout that names each pattern mutation as the engine applies it. It follows Strand 1 and redraws only when a mutation fires — between events it stays still. Whenever a parameter's Fix toggle is unlocked and that parameter drifts, the Monitor reports what the drift just did.

Each event is shown on three lines: the operation that fired, the result (which memory cell received which note, or the old → new value), and the rule that chose it.

OperationRuleWhat happens
ROTATEgate N/8The pattern shifts one step. Rotations are Euclidean-distributed across the drift window: N of every 8 cycles carry a shift, and N rises with the Evolve rate.
PULSE +longest gapA beat is added in the middle of the longest silent gap, its pitch interpolated from the neighbouring beats. The result names the filled cell and its new note.
PULSE −tightest gapThe most crowded beat — the one with the smallest gap to its neighbours — is removed. The result names the emptied cell.
STEPSsteps driftThe pattern length changes by one. The result shows the old → new step count.
TURINGrandom pitchAn active beat's pitch jumps to a new scale degree. This is the one mutation chosen at random rather than by rule, so it is drawn in amber while the others are green. The result shows the old → new note.

On the left, the ring plots the current pattern around a circle: step 0 at the top (marked by the triangle), active beats as filled nodes joined by a line, rests as faint dots. After a PULSE + the gap it just filled is highlighted. Along the bottom, the memory bar lays the steps out left to right; the cell named in the result line (M00, M01, …) flashes as it is overwritten.

Polyphonic Mode — Strands & Pitch Field

GEN runs up to five parallel Strands (Strand 1 is always active; Strands 2–5 enable individually). Each strand owns its own Euclidean pattern (steps, pulses, rotation, mutation, fix flags), an octave shift, a tempo multiplier, and a textural Role. All strands run the same internal pipeline as Strand 1 (Euclidean rhythm → Turing-machine degree walk → quantization), differing only in their parameters and their pitch source: Strand 1 quantizes to the global Scale, Strands 2–5 draw from the shared Pitch Field. Four secondary strands plus the reference voice means each of the four roles (Anchor / Line / Density / Gesture) can be assigned to its own strand alongside Strand 1, without having to drop one. Inter-strand interaction is governed by the Coordination Mode (see below) — not by per-event harmonic checks inside each strand.

The result is closer to a small ensemble than to a chord arpeggiator: rhythms cross-relate, registers separate, and the pitch material drifts as a whole.

15. Presets

akróasys uses .t5p preset files — a binary container that stores:

  • Every parameter value — over three hundred of them
  • Which oscillator was active
  • T5 Oscillator: both impulse texts, the generated audio itself (PCM, so the preset sounds right without re-generating), the inference cache capacity and any cached audio entries, the source-control vectors (768 floats each, for DimExplorer context), the seed, the device and the model selection
  • LRO: the prompt, the authored Csound orchestra, the instrument's own knobs with their values, and the authoring trace — a recalled preset compiles and plays without calling the language model again
  • Sequencer one-shot samples, including their per-step slot state

Presets carry tags — a set of descriptive labels you assign when saving. Tags are shown as chips in the save dialog; when saving over an existing preset, the chips are pre-filled from that target preset's tag set, not from whatever tags you last used. Tags are stored in the .t5p file and indexed by the Library for filtering.

Presets live in the presets folder of akróasys's app-data directory; a subfolder there is a bank, and appears as one in the Library. Right-clicking an entry offers Rename, Duplicate, Show in Finder and Delete. Use Import Presets in the Library to copy one or more .t5p files into your user library. Update Library downloads and refreshes the curated UCDCAE AI Lab bank in place from T5ynth-Presets — no manual download.

16. Session Log: Recording & Replay

akróasys can keep a session log of what you do — never of the rendered audio. It is off by default: switch on Record Event Log (.t5evt) on the Settings tab, and from then on it records every note with its source (MIDI keyboard, computer keyboard, Step Sequencer, Generative Sequencer, Arpeggiator), every parameter move (and whether it came from your hand, from a learned CC, or from host automation), and every generation call with its prompts, settings and realized seed.

The Export menu in the status bar holds both ends of it. Save Session Log… writes the session so far as a .t5evt file — newline-delimited JSON, one event per line, readable in any text editor; the entry is enabled once something has actually happened. Play Session Log… picks a .t5evt and plays it back: a full-surface REPLAY transport covers the workspace, with the tape's filename, position, a speed control, a timeline strip showing the note scatter and generation marks, what is sounding now, and a feed of recent events. The tape drives the engine underneath — parameters move, notes play, and generations are re-created live from their logged prompts and seeds rather than played back as audio. Stop Replay ends the tape; your own workspace beneath was never touched.

Because a replayed generation is re-run, not re-heard, a tape is faithful to what was done, not byte-identical to what was heard: the same prompts and seed on the same model reproduce the sound, while a missing model or a changed install renders what the machine makes of it today.

17. Strategies for Finding Sweet Spots

akróasys is not a sweet-spot synth. Unlike subtractive synths where most parameter combinations produce usable sound, akróasys's generation process is exploratory and unpredictable. Many combinations produce nothing interesting. That's by design — the instrument rewards patient exploration, not preset-surfing.

With the T5 Oscillator

1. Start with the source

Get a generation you like before touching synth parameters. A great filter envelope cannot save a bad generation. Experiment with A/B texts, seeds, and short durations (1–3s) to quickly iterate.

2. Use the fastest engine while you search

Stable Audio Open Small and the SA3 Small tiers converge in 8 steps and turn a prompt around in a fraction of the time Stable Audio Open 1.0 takes. Explore on one of those; move to a heavier checkpoint once you know the direction is worth it. Short durations (1–3 s) help more than anything else here.

3. Exploit the seed

Same A/B text + different seed = very different audio. If a marker feels right but the result doesn't, change the seed before changing the wording. Generate 5–10 seeds before concluding a marker doesn't work.

4. A/B for morphing, not mixing

The A/B slider is not a volume crossfade. It moves through the model's latent space — the midpoint (0.0) is often not a blend of A and B but something new. Use A/B to discover unexpected sounds between your two impulses. Push it past ±1.0 to extrapolate. If you leave one text field empty, A/B sweeps from your impulse through the model-neutral midpoint to its antipode — the Echoraum trajectory, sounds the impulse actively isn't.

5. One axis at a time

Sound-character axes are most effective used individually. Stacking three axes at extreme values dilutes each one. Start with one axis and learn its response before adding a second.

6. Sampler, Wavetable, and Granular as different pitch behaviours

Sampler mode follows MIDI with pitch-preserving transposition and keeps the generated fragment's full temporal character. Wavetable mode extracts pitch-synchronous frames for a more oscillator-like pitch response. Granular mode holds a local moment as a smooth sustain texture. Choose by sound and motion, not because only one of them can play pitch.

7. Short loops + Scan modulation

Generate 1–2s of audio, switch to Wavetable, and modulate Scan with a slow LFO or Env1. This creates evolving timbres from a single generation — the classic wavetable synthesis approach, but with AI-generated waves.

8. Drift for evolution, not randomness

Set Drift LFO rates very low (0.01–0.05 Hz) and depths moderate (0.2–0.5). Target A/B or an Axis. Enable Auto-regen. This activates the asynchronous feedback loop — the synth slowly transforms itself, producing a continuous stream of new generations. Far more interesting than random Noise injection.

With the LRO

9. Name a body and a movement, not a genre

The library the model consults is organised by how a sound is made and how it moves. "Struck metal bar, bowed, slowly detuning" reaches something; "cinematic" reaches nothing. If the trace's LOOKED UP station says no word of your prompt is in the library, the model is writing from its own knowledge alone — sometimes exactly what you want, but it is worth knowing which of the two you got.

10. Read the trace before rewriting the prompt

THOUGHT tells you what the machine decided your sound is. When the result is wrong, it is usually wrong there and not in the code — fix the misreading rather than adding adjectives. A REPAIRED station with several rounds is a sign the instrument was near the edge of what the model could write; a simpler description often lands better than a longer one.

11. Play the knobs before regenerating

An authored instrument usually has more range under its own knobs than a second authoring run would give you, and moving them costs nothing. Re-author when you want a different instrument, not a different setting.

12. If it does not move, ask for movement

Every LRO instrument is meant to move on its own. If one stands still, the trace's count line will usually have said so ("no movement named") — name the motion you want and write it again.

With either

13. Gen Seq + Arp as a discovery engine

Turn on the generative sequencer with moderate mutation (0.3–0.5), pick a scale, and let it run. Add the arpeggiator in Up mode at 1/16. The combination reveals how your sound behaves across pitch ranges and rhythmic contexts — a rapid audition tool.

14. Save early, save often

A preset carries the sound itself — the generated audio on the T5 side, the authored Csound on the LRO side — so a good result is never lost. Save the moment you find something promising. Synth parameters can be refined later; a generation is hard to reproduce without its exact seed and settings, and an authoring run is never reproduced exactly at all.

18. How Semantic Axes Work

akróasys's semantic axes are directions in the 768-dimensional embedding space of the T5 text encoder. Here's how they are constructed:

The construction below describes the T5-encoder engines (Stable Audio Open 1.0, Stable Audio Open Small, AudioLDM2). SA3 conditions on a different encoder (t5gemma) and builds its own three axes from synonym-bundled poles rather than single words — see the SA3 note at the end of this section.

Step 1: Define Poles

Each axis is defined by two single-word anchors representing opposing concepts — for example, "noise" and "music". These are not arbitrary labels; they were selected through systematic testing of which word-pairs produce the most audibly distinct results.

Step 2: Encode Poles + Neutral

Three embeddings are computed via the T5 encoder:

  • emb_pole_a — encoding of the negative pole (e.g., "noise")
  • emb_pole_b — encoding of the positive pole (e.g., "music")
  • emb_neutral — encoding of an empty text field ("")

Step 3: Compute Direction

The axis direction is the difference between a pole and the neutral point:

  • Positive values: direction = emb_pole_b − emb_neutral
  • Negative values: direction = emb_pole_a − emb_neutral

This direction vector is then scaled by the slider value and added to the current manipulated embedding.

Step 4: Validation

Not all conceptual oppositions produce audible differences. The 8 axes included in akróasys were validated by generating audio at each pole and measuring Mel-spectrogram cosine distance. Only axes with a distance above 0.70 at 1-second duration survived. PCA-based axes (derived from variance in the embedding space rather than semantic opposition) were tested but found to collapse at short durations — they produce near-identical audio regardless of slider position.

In the code, axis direction vectors are summed onto the embedding. But because the diffusion model processes the resulting embedding non-linearly, the audible effect is not simply additive — multiple axes are interrelated and can interfere with each other unpredictably. There is no orthogonalization, and axes may partially overlap in embedding space.

SA3: Bundled Poles

On SA3's t5gemma encoder a single-word pole is too weak to move the sound — mean-pooled across a sequence that is mostly padding, the direction nearly vanishes. SA3's three axes therefore build each pole from a stack of near-synonyms — for example "a sustained, continuous, persistent, held, unbroken, droning tone" against "a rhythmic, percussive, syncopated, pulsing, staccato, beat-driven groove" — which carries enough real tokens to survive. The edit is confined to those real tokens, so it steers without swamping your prompt.

The push is strong but not literal: it shifts the character, it does not guarantee the named property. Treat the SA3 axes as broad directions to explore by ear, not as measured controls.

19. The DimExplorer In Depth

The DimExplorer (see chapter 3 for basic usage) operates on the mean-pooled encoder output — the 768-dimensional vector obtained by averaging across the token sequence dimension. This is the same representation the diffusion model uses for conditioning.

Why Sorted by Magnitude?

Sorting the 768 bars by |emb_A[i] − emb_B[i]| is not just a display convenience — it creates two distinct editing zones:

  • Left side (high magnitude) — dimensions where A and B differ most. Editing here affects what distinguishes your two impulses from each other.
  • Right side (low magnitude) — dimensions where A and B are nearly identical, i.e., what they have in common. Editing here tends to produce more fundamental changes to the overall character of the sound, since you are altering the shared foundation rather than the A/B contrast.

What Does a Dimension Represent?

Individual T5 dimensions do not correspond to human-interpretable audio features. The T5 model was trained on language tasks (not audio), and its internal structure encodes statistical patterns in text — not timbral qualities, pitch, or rhythm. The Stable Audio model learned to map these text patterns to sound, but the mapping is opaque and non-linear. A single dimension might contribute to brightness in one A/B context and to rhythmic density in another. This is why the DimExplorer is a research tool: it rewards experimentation, not prediction.

Technical Details

  • Offsets are applied as manipulated[:, :, idx] += offset_value — broadcast across all tokens in the sequence
  • Only non-zero offsets are transmitted to the inference engine (sparse representation)
  • Offsets persist across generations until explicitly reset
  • The edit history (undo/redo) stores complete offset state snapshots

20. Principles of the Generative Sequencer

The generative sequencer combines three layers of musical algorithm:

Layer 1: Euclidean Rhythm

The rhythmic backbone uses Bjorklund's algorithm (Toussaint, 2005) to distribute N pulses across M steps as evenly as possible. This is the same mathematical principle behind many traditional rhythmic patterns worldwide — it produces patterns that feel natural because they maximize spacing regularity.

Example: 5 pulses in 8 steps → [x . x . x . x x] — a pattern equivalent to Cuban tresillo.

The Rotation parameter shifts this pattern circularly, changing which beat feels like "one."

Layer 2: Stride-Based Melody

Pulses are assigned pitches using a stride permutation through the selected scale. The stride value (max(1, steps − pulses)) determines how many scale degrees to skip between successive notes, chosen to be coprime with the total number of degrees so the melody visits as many pitches as possible before repeating.

The traversal follows a triangle wave (ascending then descending), creating bidirectional melodic motion rather than monotonic climbing. This produces melodies that feel more natural and song-like.

Velocity is derived from the rhythmic gaps — notes after longer rests receive higher velocity, creating natural accents at the start of rhythmic phrases.

Layer 3: Turing Machine Mutation

Each time the pattern completes a cycle, Mutation determines how many notes change. At mutation rate 0.8 with 16 pulses, roughly 8 notes shift by 1–4 scale degrees. The result: the pattern stays recognizable but continuously evolves — like a melodic idea being rethought in real time.

When the Mutation Fix toggle is unlocked, the mutation amount itself breathes over a fixed internal 16-cycle sine period. The Evolve slider sets the base amount; the breathing then moves around that value. This is separate from the Pitch Field Rate control, which is user-settable from 1 to 32 cycles.

Parameter Drift

When the Fix toggles are unlocked, the Euclidean parameters themselves drift over 8-cycle windows:

  • Rotation shifts circularly (timing determined by a secondary Euclidean distribution)
  • Pulses increase or decrease over time, changing pattern density
  • Steps expand or contract while preserving the pulse-to-step ratio

All drift is bounded and reversible — parameters oscillate rather than running away. The combination of melodic mutation, mutation breathing, and parameter drift produces patterns that can evolve for minutes without becoming monotonous or losing coherence.

Layer 4: Polyphonic Strands

Up to five Strands run in parallel, each with its own Euclidean DNA, drift state, role, and tempo multiplier. Strands are not voices in a chord — they are independent agents sharing one pitch space. The polyrhythmic interplay arises naturally from the sorted Div× multipliers (1/16×, 1/8×, 1/5×, 1/4×, 1/3×, 1/2×, 1×, 2×, 3×, 4×, 5×, 8×, 16×), which create non-coincident downbeats; cycle boundaries only realign on the lowest common multiple. Each strand runs the same internal pipeline as Strand 1 (Euclidean rhythm + Turing degree-walk + quantization to its register); the role choice only sets default character. How strands interact (or don't) is decided by the Coordination Mode; see chapter 12. With four secondary strands available, every role (Anchor, Line, Density, Gesture) can be assigned uniquely if desired.

Each strand's Role selects a different note-picking strategy from the shared field:

  • Anchor — low-register support. Walks the field via its degree pattern; Gravity probabilistically snaps the note to centerPc at the top of every other cycle (cadential anchor, not a per-beat tic).
  • Line — mid-register voice. Walks the field from its degree pattern.
  • Density — mid-register, shorter gate. On weak steps may take a single chromatic step toward the next field-member landing; the strong-step note remains a field member.
  • Gesture — wider register. Sparse pulses on its own Euclidean pattern.

The Gravity parameter (per strand) controls how strongly the strand returns to centerPc at cycle-pair boundaries. At dominance 0 the strand follows its degree walk freely; at 1.0 the cycle-start note becomes centerPc. There is no per-event harmonic check between strands — that responsibility is moved to the Coordination Mode.

Layer 5: The Pitch Field

All strands draw from a single shared Pitch Field — a 12-bit pitch-class set (pcSet) plus an ordered row used by Transform mode. The field replaces classical chord/scale context with something more flexible: an evolving collection of allowed pitch classes that can transform through several distinct algorithmic paradigms.

The field evolves on Strand 1's cycle boundary at the chosen Rate (1–32 cycles between changes). Four modes:

  • Static — derived from Scale Root + Scale Type, never changes. Use when you want the harmonic field stable while only rhythm and mutation evolve.
  • Drift — each tick, one pitch class is removed and one chromatic pitch class is added (the center pc is protected from removal). Slow harmonic motion that stays close to the starting set.
  • Transform — applies a random twelve-tone row operation each tick: transposition (Tn), inversion (In), retrograde (R), or retrograde-inversion (RI). The new pcSet is then derived from the transformed row's first rowSize members.
  • Pivot — transposes the entire pc-set by the configured Pivot interval (m3, M3, Tritone, P5) each tick, accumulating shift over time. After enough cycles the field returns to its origin (e.g. four cycles at m3 = full octave).

Because all strands share the same field, harmonic coherence is provided at the field level alone: a Drift swap or Transform tick changes what every strand can play. There is no per-event harmonic check between strands — that question is delegated to the Coordination Mode (chapter 12), which currently caps simultaneous voices via priority displacement (DensityBudget). The listener perceives one evolving texture because the strands draw from the same evolving pitch set, not because each note is negotiated against the others.

Roles, dominance, and field modes operate without a Bass/Lead/Pad hierarchy or chord-root tracking. The intent is to produce evolving textures independent of functional harmony. For diatonic results, set Field Mode = Static, Gravity high, and one strand to Anchor.

22. How Injection Modes Work

The user-facing injection-mode buttons (chapter 1) are a thin UI on top of two specific manipulation points inside the Stable Audio Open diffusion pipeline. This section explains those points carefully so the modes are predictable rather than mysterious.

What "injection" means here

The Stable Audio Open diffusion transformer (DiT) consumes the text-conditioning embedding via cross-attention: every one of its 16 transformer blocks contains a cross_attn layer that mixes the audio latent with a projected version of the T5-encoded text. The cross-attention conditioning tensor enters the DiT once per sampler step, gets projected through a to_cond_embed layer, and is then fed identically to every block.

"Injection" in akróasys means: instead of conditioning the DiT with a single static A↔B blend, we manipulate the conditioning tensor along two orthogonal axes:

  1. Time (sampler steps) — replace the conditioning at a specific sampler step. Early steps see one tensor; late steps see another. This is the "Step-in" axis.
  2. Depth (DiT block index) — replace the conditioning per block. Different blocks see different tensors at the same time. This is the "Layer" axis.

The Combo modes combine both axes simultaneously: at a given step, each of the 16 blocks may see a different mix.

The two PyTorch hooks that do the work

Both axes are implemented as Python-level monkey-patches and hooks applied to the loaded diffusion pipeline before sampling, then removed at the end of each generation. The C++ side never sees them — it only sets the mode string and parameters in the request.

  • Sampler-step counter — the pipeline's DiTWrapper.forward is replaced with a closure that increments a counter state["calls"] after every call. Because the wrapper is invoked exactly once per sampler step (with both CFG halves batched), the counter equals the current step index. Late-step modes compare it against transition_step = max(1, round(steps · injection_transition_at)) to decide whether the early or late conditioning is active.
  • Per-block forward_pre_hooks on each block.cross_attn. The hook intercepts the runtime context kwarg (the to_cond_embed-projected conditioning, doubled for CFG) and returns a replacement tensor. The replacement is pre-projected through the same to_cond_embed layer once at request start, so the hook bypasses projection and just swaps the post-projection context.

Why hard mask in Combo but soft mask in Layer

Layer mode lets the user drag a two-thumb range slider, and a sigmoid top-hat (soft 4-layer ramps at each edge) means small slider movements produce continuous changes — a gentler control surface for live exploration. Combo modes, by contrast, are presets: the band is fixed by the mode itself, the user only controls the intensity slider. There is nothing to interpolate. A hard mask (w=1 inside the band, 0 outside) makes "slider=1" mean exactly what users expect — 100 % B in those specific blocks. Empirically, a 4-block-wide Layer band peaks at w≈0.534 in the centre with the soft mask; the hard mask version of the same 4 blocks reads as a far stronger preset effect even at moderate slider settings.

Why these particular layer bands

The cross-attention layers in the SA Open Small DiT are not interchangeable — they appear to perform different work at different depths. The current Combo tuples (Combo 1 = blocks 0..3, Combo 2 = blocks 4..11, Combo 3 = blocks 6..9) reflect listening-test findings:

  • Surface (low blocks) — narrow effect. Influences the surface "skin" / texture of the output without changing its overall shape much. This is the Combo 1 band.
  • Mid (broad) — high conditioning leverage. The middle 8 blocks carry most of the diffusion's text-following work, so injecting B there reshapes the result substantially. This is the Combo 2 band.
  • High (top 4) — surprisingly low leverage. The original plan placed Combo 2 at blocks 12..15 to mirror Combo 1's "skin" framing, but listening tests revealed the top blocks are dominated by refinement work and barely register cross-attention overrides. Combo 2 was moved to broad-mid.
  • Center (narrow) — same width as Combo 1 at a different vertical position. This is Combo 3, the "where-versus-width" probe.

These bands are subject to revision as more listening data accrues. The backend/pipe_inference.py handler is the authoritative source; the C++ side sends matching values purely for log/preset legibility.

What stays unchanged

  • Magnitude, Noise, Dimension Offsets, Semantic Axes all reach the DiT in every mode. They are applied to whichever embedding tensor(s) the active mode actually feeds into the cross-attention layer — the global blend in Linear, both the early A path and the late blend in Step-in, both A and B (independently, with the same shifts) before projection in Layer, and A and the late blend before projection in Combo 1/2/3. The same noise sample and the same axis / dim shifts are reused across paths so the per-mode geometry behaves predictably (e.g. a Linear-style A↔B blend would still receive noise once, not per term).
  • Drift targets the active mode's intensity axis automatically. No new drift targets are needed.
  • Determinism — the hooks are deterministic; they do not introduce additional RNG. Same seed + same A/B text + same mode and slider position = identical audio across platforms.
  • Linear remains the canonical default. The non-linear modes are research controls layered on top of it.

Huovilainen Ladder Filter Reference

Reference: Antti Huovilainen, "Non-linear digital implementation of the ladder filter", Proc. of the 7th Int. Conference on Digital Audio Effects (DAFx-04), Naples, 2004.
Paper: dafx.de/paper-archive/2004/P_061.PDF

The Ladder filter algorithm is an original implementation of the non-linear digital ladder described in Huovilainen 2004 — half-sample-delay-compensated 4-pole with tanh at each stage input and in the feedback path. DSP algorithms are not themselves copyrightable; credit for the topology and its proper digital formulation belongs to Antti Huovilainen. Reference implementations that informed our understanding (no code copied): chowdsp_utils (BSD-3), Vital (GPL-3), Surge XT (GPL-3).

Cutoff Warp Filter — design inspiration

Reference: Surge XT src/common/dsp/filters/CutoffWarp.cpp  ·  License of reference: GNU GPLv3
URL: github.com/surge-synthesizer/surge.

The Warp filter algorithm (src/dsp/CutoffWarpFilter.h) is an original implementation inspired by the Cutoff Warp filter pioneered in Surge XT. The ZDF 4-pole ladder topology is a standard digital filter formulation and not copyrightable in itself; the specific idea of a style-switchable per-feedback nonlinearity as a musical character parameter is credited to the Surge XT project. No Surge XT source code was copied — our implementation is written from scratch against the published ZDF ladder derivation, and our saturation set (Tanh / SoftClip / Algebraic / Sin-fold / Digital / Asym) is a musically curated subset rather than a verbatim reproduction of Surge's full style list.
